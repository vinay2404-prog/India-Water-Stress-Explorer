# 💧 India Water Stress Explorer

An interactive, district-level groundwater and water-stress assessment application for India, built using Python, Streamlit, Pandas, NumPy and Plotly.

The project combines historical groundwater-level observations from the **Central Ground Water Board (CGWB)** with district-level rainfall normals from the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)** to examine groundwater conditions, estimate recharge, calculate a Water Stress Index (WSI), and compare water-stress indicators across districts.

The application provides interactive visualizations, district rankings, groundwater trend analysis, rainfall comparisons, and configurable model parameters.

---

## 📌 Project Overview

Groundwater is an important source of water for agriculture, domestic consumption and industrial activities in India. Understanding groundwater-level changes and rainfall conditions can help identify districts experiencing relatively higher groundwater stress.

This project develops a data-driven framework to:

- Analyze historical groundwater-level observations.
- Estimate groundwater depletion and recovery trends.
- Estimate seasonal groundwater recharge using the Water Table Fluctuation (WTF) method.
- Examine annual rainfall and monsoon rainfall distribution.
- Calculate a composite Water Stress Index.
- Classify districts into Low, Moderate, High and Critical stress categories.
- Explore district-level groundwater conditions through an interactive dashboard.

**Important:** The WSI developed in this project is a relative, project-defined composite index. It is not an official government water-stress classification or a complete measure of water scarcity.

## 📊 Data Sources

The project uses two primary datasets.

| Dataset | Source | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Groundwater-level observations | Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) | Historical groundwater-level analysis |
| District rainfall normals | India Meteorological Department (IMD) | Annual and monsoon rainfall analysis |

### Groundwater Data

The groundwater dataset contains monitoring-station observations covering 1970–2025.

The analysis uses:

- April and May measurements for pre-monsoon groundwater depth.
- November measurements for post-monsoon groundwater depth.
- Available observations from 2010 onward for groundwater trend estimation.
- Recent observations from 2021–2025 for district-level groundwater indicators.

### Rainfall Data

The IMD district rainfall normals dataset provides:

- Annual rainfall normals.
- June–September rainfall normals.

Rainfall data are matched with groundwater districts using state and district names, including fuzzy name matching and state-level fallback where necessary.

## ⚙️ Data Processing Workflow

The project follows a two-stage workflow.

### Stage 1: Data Preparation

The `src/build_data.py` script:

1. Loads CGWB groundwater-level observations.
2. Standardizes state and district names.
3. Converts monthly groundwater observations into a long-format dataset.
4. Removes missing and invalid groundwater readings.
5. Extracts year and month information.
6. Calculates pre-monsoon and post-monsoon groundwater levels.
7. Estimates seasonal groundwater-level rise.
8. Calculates station-level groundwater depletion trends.
9. Aggregates monitoring-station measurements at district level.
10. Matches IMD rainfall normals with groundwater districts.
11. Exports processed district and station datasets.

### Stage 2: Interactive Analysis

The `app.py` application:

1. Loads the processed CSV datasets.
2. Applies the selected specific-yield value.
3. Estimates groundwater recharge.
4. Calculates recharge ratios and monsoon rainfall shares.
5. Normalizes the four stress components.
6. Calculates the Water Stress Index.
7. Classifies districts according to the selected thresholds.
8. Displays interactive charts, rankings and district-level results.

## 🧮 Hydrological Methodology

### 1. Groundwater Depletion Rate

Groundwater trends are estimated using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression of pre-monsoon groundwater depth against year.

\[
Rate =
\frac{n\sum xy-\sum x\sum y}
{n\sum x^2-(\sum x)^2}
\]

Where:

- \(x\) = year.
- \(y\) = pre-monsoon groundwater depth.
- \(n\) = number of valid observations.

**Interpretation:**

- Positive rate: groundwater depth is increasing, indicating a deeper water table.
- Negative rate: groundwater depth is decreasing, indicating a shallower water table.
- Zero rate: no linear change in groundwater depth.

The trend is calculated using available observations from 2010 onward, subject to the minimum valid-observation requirement.

### 2. Seasonal Groundwater Rise

\[
\Delta h = \max(D_{pre}-D_{post},0)
\]

Where:

- \(D_{pre}\) = pre-monsoon groundwater depth.
- \(D_{post}\) = post-monsoon groundwater depth.

Groundwater depth is measured in metres below ground level.

### 3. Groundwater Recharge Estimation

The Water Table Fluctuation method is used to estimate gross recharge:

\[
R=S_y\times\Delta h\times1000
\]

Where:

- \(R\) = estimated recharge in mm.
- \(S_y\) = specific yield.
- \(\Delta h\) = seasonal groundwater rise in metres.

Specific yield is configurable through the dashboard.

### 4. Recharge Ratio

\[
Recharge\ Ratio =
\frac{R}{P_{annual}}\times100
\]

Where:

- \(R\) = estimated groundwater recharge.
- \(P_{annual}\) = annual rainfall normal.

The result represents estimated recharge relative to annual rainfall. It should not be interpreted as a directly measured infiltration percentage.

### 5. Monsoon Rainfall Share

\[
Monsoon\ Share =
\frac{P_{JJAS}}{P_{annual}}\times100
\]

Where:

- \(P_{JJAS}\) = June–September rainfall.
- \(P_{annual}\) = annual rainfall.

This measures the proportion of annual rainfall occurring during the monsoon months.

## 📈 Water Stress Index (WSI)

The project develops a composite Water Stress Index using four indicators.

| Component | Indicator | Higher stress represents |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | Pre-monsoon groundwater depth | Deeper groundwater table |
| Trend | Groundwater depletion rate | Faster groundwater-level decline |
| Rainfall | Annual rainfall | Lower annual rainfall |
| Recharge | Recharge ratio | Lower estimated recharge relative to rainfall |

### 1. Indicator Normalization

To combine indicators measured in different units, percentile-based min-max normalization is applied.

\[
z =
\operatorname{clip}
\left(
\frac{x-P_5}{P_{95}-P_5},0,1
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(x\) = indicator value.
- \(P_5\) = fifth percentile.
- \(P_{95}\) = ninety-fifth percentile.

For rainfall and recharge ratio, the normalized values are inverted because lower values represent greater stress.

### 2. WSI Calculation

\[
WSI =
100\times
\frac{
w_dC_d+w_tC_t+w_rC_r+w_cC_c
}{
w_d+w_t+w_r+w_c
}
\]

Where:

- \(C_d\) = normalized groundwater-depth component.
- \(C_t\) = normalized depletion-trend component.
- \(C_r\) = normalized rainfall-deficit component.
- \(C_c\) = normalized recharge-deficit component.
- \(w_d,w_t,w_r,w_c\) = component weights.

### Default WSI Weights

| Component | Default weight |
|---|---:|
| Groundwater depth | 0.35 |
| Depletion rate | 0.30 |
| Rainfall deficit | 0.20 |
| Recharge deficit | 0.15 |
| **Total** | **1.00** |

The weights are configurable through the application sidebar.

Normalization is performed using all districts in the loaded district dataset before the user applies state and station-count filters. This keeps the normalization reference population consistent when exploring different subsets.

### WSI Classification

| WSI Score | Category |
|---:|---|
| Below 35 | Low |
| 35 to below 50 | Moderate |
| 50 to below 65 | High |
| 65–100 | Critical |

These are project-defined classification thresholds and can be modified through the dashboard.

## ⚠️ Data Quality and Research Limitations

The results should be interpreted in light of the following limitations.

### 1. Relative Water Stress Index

The WSI is a relative composite index based on the districts included in the dataset. It does not directly measure absolute water scarcity or water availability.

### 2. Specific Yield Assumption

Recharge estimates depend strongly on the selected specific-yield value. The current application applies one user-selected value across all districts, although aquifer characteristics can differ geographically.

### 3. Simplified Recharge Estimation

The WTF estimate does not explicitly account for all groundwater inflows, outflows or monsoon pumping effects.

### 4. Rainfall Matching

District rainfall values are matched using district names, fuzzy name matching and state-level averages where necessary. State-average matches have lower geographic precision.

### 5. Different Reference Periods

Recent groundwater measurements and IMD rainfall normals may represent different reference periods. Their comparison should not be interpreted as a time-matched causal analysis.

### 6. Monitoring Network Changes

Groundwater monitoring stations may change over time. District trends can therefore be influenced by changes in station availability and coverage.

### 7. Missing Extraction and Population Data

The current framework does not include groundwater extraction, population, irrigation demand or aquifer-specific storage data.

Consequently, it cannot calculate official groundwater extraction stages or comprehensive per-capita water availability indicators.

### 8. Linear Trend Scenarios

Future groundwater depth estimates assume that the historical linear trend continues unchanged. They are illustrative scenarios, not reliable forecasts.

### 9. Physical Well Depth

The estimated time to reach recorded physical well depth is a simplified calculation. Physical well depth is not necessarily the critical groundwater level or the pumping limit of the aquifer.

### 10. WSI Weights and Thresholds

The default weights and classification cut-offs are project assumptions. Different choices can change district scores and categories. Sensitivity analysis is recommended.
